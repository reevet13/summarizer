At the same time, houses have gone from affordable loans with payoff structures of 5-10 years to 'maybe when you retire, maybe if your kids pay it off'.

You can't move to rural areas, there's no jobs. You're stuck in the city, with a huge commute and the desperate hope that the landlord doesn't raise the rent by the maximum again this year.

I think the biggest problem, at least in the UK, is that you are set up to be in debt. You go to uni and you have to take up a loan. I left with almost £20000 worth of debt, and that's before getting a "real" job.

I've been out of uni for almost three years, earn well above average, yet still have no disposable income because I'm still paying off debts the government's education policy forced me into.

Much of that is population growth/density changes. You have more people fighting over the same houses in key locations, so costs rise.

As long as the population continues to increase, a smaller % of people will be able to afford to live in the nicest neighborhoods.

What the people profiled (and the authors) don't seem to realize is that they chose to enter the careers they felt like entering, without any regard as to whether it was a field with any great demand, while their parents mostly entered the careers that were offered to them and provided the best wages and benefits.

In short, being a plumber may not be hip, but the world *needs* plumbers, not more of the "creative class."
