The power of the House of Lords is [severely restricted](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Lords#Legislative_functions). In addition, they have at times proved to be a useful check on the government's power in rejecting some of the more illiberal anti-terror legislation.

Honestly, sometimes I'm astounded by the difference in the degree to which people in the UK feel that government should take a role in regulating their lives. Whether it's right-wing morality laws regarding pornography on the Internet like this one or left-wing laws banning [fox hunting](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fox_hunting) or the [ownership of katanas](https://secure.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/en/wiki/Katana#Ownership_and_trade_in_the_United_Kingdom), there is clearly a different mindset in the UK. It's sort of a "we should establish a majority opinion as to what type of behavior is ideal, and then everyone should follow that" rather than the more "live and let live" approach in the United States.

It's not like that anymore. The House of Lords got purged by Labour some years ago, its reform was a major part of the party's manifesto. They got rid of the noble peers who weren't actually actively contributing to the political process, abolished hereditary peership, and set up commitees to give peerships to experts in their fields and politicans who've proved their worth to the country.

Edit - Also, I have to say I'm rather glad that the system isn't democratic, given that (scarily) it's the non-elected House of Lords that has continually attempted to safeguard civil liberties in Britain :/

The US isn't completely unrestricted either (and I'm sure it's possible to find some area where this is reversed), but on these mentioned points, at least, it is less-restrictive than the United Kingdom.

Governments are still allowed to be morality police and enforce laws "to protect the common good". The constitution has some limits on what that means, but I don't think the British system does. In fact most European constitutions are written to be as vague as possible with regards to legislative powers, but as restrictive as possible regarding executive powers, the theory being whichever government is in power legitimately represents the people. This has some shortcomings apparently, especially since anything can be criminalized easily, and France still has no "Innocent before guilty" burden of proof in its justice system.
