"Using her position as a social worker, Sendler regularly entered the ghetto, smuggling around 2,500 children out in boxes, suitcases or hidden in trolleys.

"But in 1943 Sendler, who led the children' section of the Zegota organization which helped Jews during the war, was arrested and tortured by the Gestapo. She only escaped execution when Zegota managed to bribe some Nazi officials, who left her unconscious but alive with broken legs and arms in the woods.

"She was also nominated for the Nobel Peace Prize last year but, despite her bravery, she denied she was a hero. "The term 'hero' irritates me greatly. The opposite is true. I continue to have pangs of conscience that I did so little," Sendler said in one of her last interviews."

Her story is an example of the bravery of many Polish people who risked their lives for Jewish people in WWII. Hiding a Jew in Nazi occupied Holland meant imprisonment and confiscation of all your property. In occupied Poland it was death on the spot for the entire household.

And no it wasn't some nebulous "Nazi Regime" from planet Mars who did it. It was the Germans, OK? I'm all for political correctness but not at the expense of rewriting history.

Oh, and for the record, Poles received the greatest share of the "Righteous among the nations of the world" awards. As amazing as the story of "Schindler's list" was, similar sort of bravery wasn't in short supply in the occupied Poland.

The history of Jews in Poland is pretty complicated. On one hand we do have shining examples of selflessness and bravery during the occupation, but on the other we have had bench ghettos before the war, and the two Pogrom's you have mentioned. A completly separate story is the percieved involvement of Jews in the stalinist oppression appratus after the war (which is sometimes given as a reason for the silent support of Kilece Pogrom), and the growing resentment against Jews in the whole of Europe before WW2.
